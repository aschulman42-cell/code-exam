# Claim charts: the tests behind the feature

*This page holds the measurements that stand behind
[`CODEEXAM_CLAIM_CHARTS.md`](CODEEXAM_CLAIM_CHARTS.md), moved here on 2026-11-09 so the
feature page can stay a plain account of what claim charting does and how to run it. Each
section is one test or one record: when it ran, what question it answers, what was measured
and where the full tables are. Dates and numbers are as first recorded; a rerun will differ,
and every figure is one sample per cell unless it says otherwise. The measured blocks are
regenerated by the scripts named beside them; the prose is revised by hand. Two tables of
filings are kept as tables, against this project's print rule, because their rows are links
that a list would triple in length. The terms table comes first because these pages grew
their own vocabulary; it maps each term to what a user sees on the screen or on disk.*

## The two populations, and the measured shape of a litigated claim 1 (2026-10-19)

What the claims pipeline is calibrated on, and what the frame tables measured; the feature
page keeps the one-paragraph version.

**Two patent populations.** The claims pipeline is calibrated on a corpus of about 385 software
patents that have been asserted in litigation, claim 1 with its dependent chain, drawn from the
USPTO Patent Litigation Docket Reports data (March 2024 release; cases to about 2020), with the
claim structure cross-checked against Google Patents and the USPTO weekly grant XML: on the order
of 85,249 markup-verified dependents, with about 554,000 canonical claim-1s as the wider
backdrop. A second population, from the USPTO's Artificial Intelligence Patent Dataset, covers
the subject matter CodeExam's AI/ML detectors target. These are the populations the tool is
built for and tuned on; no single example is a yardstick (a rule the work keeps: calibrate on a
population, never on the case in hand). The litigated corpus's claim-1 texts are in the
repository as
[`test/fixtures/litigated-claim1-structure.jsonl`](../test/fixtures/litigated-claim1-structure.jsonl)
(380 records, 840 KB: patent, original assignee, grant date, claim text); the full fetch with
each claim's dependent chain is written outside the repository by
`scripts/litigated-claims-fetch.mjs`. The AI-patent population's claim texts are not in the
repository; what it derives from them is: the frame counts for 841 AI/ML claim 1s of 2013 to 2017
in `src/core/claim-frame-tables.json`, the shape bands `--shape-profile ai-ml` draws on in
`src/commands/pseudo-claims.js`, and the vocabulary profile in `src/core/claim-genre-df.json`.

**The measured shape of a litigated claim 1** (frame tables, deterministic, 2026-10-19). 380 of
the 385 families yield a parseable claim-1 structure; each count below excludes the rows where
its feature is absent and names its own denominator. Of 380 structure rows: method 187, system
70, computer-readable medium 15, Jepson 35, other 73. The transition is `comprising` in 97.6% of
378 (`the steps of` tail in 21%). Of 956 method-claim rows beyond the preamble, 80.8% open with
a gerund (`receiving`, `determining`, `providing`, `generating`). Of the 3,257 dependents that
carry a reference phrase (36 do not), 72.8% use `of claim`; of the 3,210 that carry an opener (83
do not), 76.6% open with `wherein` and 17.2% with `further comprising`. These tables,
`src/core/claim-frame-tables.json`, are what `--claim-schema` enforces on a drafted pseudo-claim's
frame through `src/core/claim-schema.js`, which renders the claim object the drafter fills.

## Three tiers of test, and the negative control

**Three tiers of test**, by what kind of answer exists:

- **Structural, no model.** Does CodeExam's own element-splitter and dependency-detector
  reproduce the patent office's markup? The dependency detector disagrees with the PTO's
  parent on none of the 3,293 dependents of claim 1, with 4 unresolved
  (`claim-frame-tables.json`, `parentAgreement`); the element-splitter agrees less often and is
  being calibrated.
- **Negative controls.** Truth is `ABSENT` by construction; any `PRESENT` is a false finding.
  First chart 4 of 4 `ABSENT`.
- **Positive screening.** Retrieval only: where a patent's assignee also publishes open source,
  multisect ranks indexes by how many of a claim's distinctive terms co-occur. This measures
  retrieval, not infringement.

## Real-world claim charts that cite code (the RECAP search of 2026-10-20)

The public filings whose charts cite functions of an open-source repository: where they come
from, how they were found, the fourteen the agreement test runs on (Table A) and the further
filings outside it (Table B). Both tables are kept as tables, against the print rule, because
every row is four links.

**Why public filings.** Litigation records name an accused product, not a public codebase, so
there is no public answer key that says "this claim reads on this code". The nearest thing is a
party's own chart that cites a file or function of an open-source repository: advocacy, not an
answer key, but a real claim against real code, placed by someone with a stake. Such charts exist
because some litigation now targets open-source projects used by deep-pocketed defendants.

**Where the citations live.** In exhibits to complaints (claim charts, usually two columns),
in infringement contentions and motions about them, in expert declarations, and in complaints
that paste code into their narrative. A citation can be a line link, a file link with a function
named beside it, a file link, a document in a repository, a directory, a bare repository, or no
link at all with code quoted by name (`secp256k1_ecdsa_sign_inner()`, `main_impl.h`). Some
filings paste the code itself, as text or as a screenshot; pasted text is readable, a screenshot
carries nothing the search can read.

**The search.** `scripts/recap-claim-chart-search.mjs` runs fixed query shapes against
CourtListener's RECAP archive, which answers full-text queries over filed documents with no
account and serves the documents free; it downloads every hit, extracts the text, records every
repository citation with its granularity, and in a second pass reads code quoted by name with no
link. Sized 2026-10-20: "claim chart" with a GitHub link, 89 hits; a file-level GitHub link in a
patent-infringement filing, 188; "infringement contentions" with a file-level link, 23; "source
code" with "claim chart" and GitHub, 49; a motion to strike contentions with GitHub, 104; a
declaration with a file-level link, 164. The full run over six shapes read every hit: 161
dockets, 274 documents, 1.6 GB of PDFs, filed 2010 to 2026. Of the 274, 80 name at least one
function and 49 name three or more (33 of those with no file-level link); for 39 of the 49, the
limitation each row quotes is the patent's own claim text, fetched from Google Patents and
aligned to the row by its words (2026-10-21).

A further sweep for charts that cite source code without GitHub (131 documents) found 12 that
name code and 6 with three or more functions:
Interactive Games v. FanDuel (Android API names), Dolby v. Snap (x264 and x265 by name), Cellspin
v. Autel (a decompiled app). The catalog, the PDFs and the per-document text stay outside the
repository; [`real-world-claim-charts.md`](real-world-claim-charts.md) carries the generated
listing and the regeneration command.

**Reading the misses.** Judged by reading the code behind the litigant's rows and CodeExam's
own logs: five of the fourteen keys are not ones a claim-to-code search should be expected to
reproduce; two misses are enumeration depth, the function CodeExam found holds the lines the

- *Raw-substring word matching* (bridged 2026-10-22, below): the symbol search matched a
  predicted word as a raw substring of the name, so `reduction` could not find
  `secp256k1_scalar_reduce_512` (zero candidates for the element, on a chart whose association
  is at least arguable).
- *One-hop navigation* (second hop added 2026-10-22, below): navigation was one hop from
  model-proposed symbols only, so `CKey::Check` and `secp256k1_ec_seckey_verify`, the

  on a `test/` directory or a `Test` token, so a project that kept `tests.c` and `testutil.h`

- *A 25-per-element pool at scale* (reported, widened and tie-broken 2026-10-22, below): a pool

## The self-test (HOF), and the bottom line for claim-to-code association

The half of the testing that is not real-world. The recipe and the one-command script are on
[`CODEEXAM_PSEUDO_CLAIMS_TESTS.md`](CODEEXAM_PSEUDO_CLAIMS_TESTS.md); here is what it says
about claim charting: first the loop of 2026-11-02 and its reading, as the feature page
carried them, then the ladder across indexes and the mechanism-grain figures, then the same
test run entirely on a local model.

**The self-test, "huffing our own fumes" (HOF).** Every claim-to-code answer key in hand is
compromised: a hand-built set is tiny, `.demo` was written by a model together with its own
claims, and a real patent shares vocabulary with its own implementation, so a match can be word
overlap rather than skill. HOF manufactures an answer key on any index in three steps: draft
pseudo-claims from code with the anchors they were drafted from attached (`--pseudo-claims
--claims-only`); have a *different* model reword them away from the code's vocabulary with the
anchors withheld (`--synonymize`, the load-bearing step, since it creates the gap between claim
words and identifiers that `.demo` lacks and a real patent has); feed the reworded claims back
through `--claim-*` and score whether the same anchors resurface, assigned to reasonable
limitations. The name is the caution: CodeExam wrote the claim and then went looking, so the loop
confirms a floor of capability, not claim-to-code skill against an outside key. Its lesson
(2026-08-19, two corpora, `scripts/anchor-recall.mjs`): the single number is the wrong number.
Scored on the granularity ladder, "100% reached" was overwhelmingly the right class or the right
file; function-level recall was 5 to 8%, and for a chart a class is not a citation, because the
limitation is implemented by the method. Since 2026-11-03 `scripts/hof.mjs` runs the test
from one index name: it drafts, locates each draft with `--claim-locate`, and scores the
drafter's anchors against the located targets on that ladder, rank reported and never a
floor. The write-ups are
[`CODEEXAM_PSEUDO_CLAIMS_TESTS.md`](CODEEXAM_PSEUDO_CLAIMS_TESTS.md)'s HOF section,
[`claims-pipeline-state.md`](claims-pipeline-state.md) §4 and §9, and
[`litigated-claims-ground-truth.md`](litigated-claims-ground-truth.md); the issue was #310.
The loop runs on the drafter ceiling's real-code drafts through `scripts/ceiling-to-loop.mjs`,
which merges a repository's chosen ce-seeded groups into the loop's inputs, renumbered with
their anchors as the answer key, and carries the ceiling's control pairing over as the loop's
control index (a filing paired within the same repository gets a named fallback or no control).
Run that way on 2026-11-02 over the twelve groups the ceiling's judge read as findings, seven
repositories, the asserted claim's class, ChatGPT synonymizing and Claude Sonnet 4.6 charting
(36 charts, $22.77 with the synonymize calls, above the $10 to $15 projected because these
claims carry 3 to 16 mechanism rows): recall on the original wording mean 0.49, median 0.5, at
or above 0.5 on 8 of 12 and zero on the two secp256k1 field-normalization claims; synonymized
mean 0.46, median 0.4, below the original on 5 claims, equal on 5, above on 2; false PRESENT on
the controls 2 of 96 mechanism rows. Against the August floor on CodeExam's own index (1.0,
0.75, 0.25 original; 1.0, 0.5, 0.13 synonymized) the real-code drafts sit in the lower half with
a smaller drop under rewording. Four of the twelve close on a "whereby" clause; their last rows
scored no worse than the other eight's, too few to say more. One sample per cell; the per-claim
table is a working file outside the repository (`hof_loop_whereby_reading_110226.md`).

**Nomination against judgment (2026-11-02).** The loop's recall is a nomination rate: the share
of drafted anchors that entered the chart's analysis set at all. Over both wordings 42% of the
anchors were nominated and 27% were judged PRESENT on themselves; the two field claims
nominated nothing, every element sent to the scalar module, whose limb, reduce and overflow
vocabulary collides with the field module's. Judgment is the stronger half. Of the 125 PRESENT
verdicts on mechanism rows, 58 sit on the drafted function, 36 one call edge from one (the
contract entry point and the library it delegates to, the pool's validate and the validator it
calls, shred verify and the Merkle recovery), 17 on the class that holds the drafted methods,
12 in the same file or directory, 2 unrelated: a PRESENT lands on the inspiring function, one
call edge from it or on its class 89% of the time, and within its directory 98%, consistent
with the control's 2% false PRESENT (`hof_neighborhood_110226.mjs`, beside the reading). So the
floor holds at the verdict and gives way at nomination, which is where a litigated claim's
wording, farther from the code than the drafter's, will cost more. A file-level pointer is in
reach on precision but not yet shown to be earned: ten of the twelve claims carry a token of
their anchor file's name on both wordings ("scalar", "field", "token", "shred", "wallet
address"), and ranking the index's files by claim words in their paths alone puts the anchor
file first, alone or in a tie of at most five, on 5 of 12. Where that trivial rank fails
(DSToken 10th, shred 7th, legacypool 323rd, T5Attention unranked) the chart nominated the file
anyway; where it succeeds (the two field files, a four-way tie) the chart did not. The
litigated charts of the filings section above are the material on which a pointer can be shown
to beat the file name.

**The bottom line, across indexes (2026-11-03 to 2026-11-06).** The one-command self-test ran
on 2026-11-03 with `--limit 4` over ten indexes (the demo, CodeExam's own, Bram, sr_gh,
GhostArch, AI_Manning, ffmpeg, x264, WinAPI_Classic, and Spinellis on 11-05), Claude drafting
and locating, one run each; the per-index ladder is on
[#359](https://github.com/aschulman42-cell/code-exam/issues/359). The reading that holds
across them: the file is reached and the function is not, every element on a real-size index
sits at the 25-candidate cap, and the shape is the same for Claude and ChatGPT. Scored again
at the mechanism grain on the same claims ([`describe-reach.md`](describe-reach.md),
2026-11-05 and 11-06), pooled over eight indexes, 32 claims and 332 anchors:

| route from the drafted claim to its code | anchor's mechanism first | within 25 | note |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| `--claim-locate`, Claude, the function rung | 35% | | the shipped locator, on the seven indexes where it ran; file rung or better 74% |
| labels and member names, the shipped embedding model, no LLM | 61% | 96% | best chunk of 8 names per entry |
| the same, then a model choosing among each element's five nearest | 84% | | ceiling 85%; $0.47 for 32 claims; does not transfer to the litigants' claims |

The litigants' claims at the same grain, the honest test: the entry holding a cited function
within 25 for 58% of 114 names over the fourteen filings, with no describer and no LLM. The
gap that remains there is register, not vocabulary.

**Gemma 4, locally (asus-CC on #363, 2026-11-07 to 2026-11-09; not re-run here).** Everything
below ran on a 16 GB GPU laptop with no cloud call: Gemma 4 12B Q4_K_M drafted and located,
the shipped embedding model scored. Figures are as asus-CC posted them on
[#363](https://github.com/aschulman42-cell/code-exam/issues/363), cited by each comment's
UTC time.

- **Drafting** (2026-11-08 06:32). 12 groups per index, drawn at random from ExoPlayer3 and
  sr_gh with mostly-test groups excluded: 24 of 24 claims drafted, 23 with grounded anchors,
  no runaway, the second request for the anchors block load-bearing on sr_gh (7 of 12 first
  replies had none). An earlier pilot on CodeExam's own index (2026-11-07) had 1 claim of 4
  run away.
- **Round trip, 88 anchors pooled** (the same comment). Gemma's own `--claim-locate` reached
  the drafted function for 57% and the file or better for 86%, listing 189 targets for 34
  anchors on ExoPlayer3 and 341 for 54 on sr_gh; the embedding route put the anchor's
  mechanism first for 58% and within 25 for 98%, against 6% and 25% for a shuffled control;
  BM25 on the same text 56% and 82%, collapsing on ExoPlayer3 where the embedding held.
- **Per element** (the same comment). The mechanism grain cannot tell one element's code from
  a sibling element's (tied on 119 of 122 pairs), and the locator lists an element's own
  anchor under that element for 6 of 61 and 22 of 80 elements; both are questions of grain
  and attribution, not of model. Dependents carry no anchors of their own.
- **Literalness** (2026-11-08 19:45 and 20:20). The round trips that worked were claims in the
  code's own vocabulary whose anchors are one class or file; the failures were near-duplicate
  files the file-keyed scorer counts as misses, a generic claim with an off-target anchor, and
  misattributed anchors. The less literal a Gemma claim, the worse its locator round trip,
  modestly (correlation 0.47); the embedding route barely cares (0.19). No Gemma claim leaves
  the engineer's register, so these runs test the floor and not the register gap. The Claude
  claims of 2026-11-03, measured the same way from this side (2026-11-08 20:44), are less
  literal (median 49% against about 69%) and their round trip depends on it even less (0.30
  with the embedding's rank 1, 0.15 with the locator's function rung).
- **Descriptions as the matched text, on sr_gh** (2026-11-09 01:10 to 01:35). With Gemma's
  own claims, Gemma-written descriptions beat the labels at rank 1 (74% against 61% under the
  embedding, 94% against 70% under BM25), and so did Gemini's (70%); with Claude's four claims
  the labels won (78% against 61% under either describer), because near-duplicate modules get
  near-identical sentences whoever writes them. Within 5, every text sits at the 90% ceiling.
